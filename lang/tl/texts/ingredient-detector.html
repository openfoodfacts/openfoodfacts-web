Vegan Detection

Strictly Plant-Based

Beyond obvious meat, poultry, and dairy, our analyzer flags covert animal-derived substances that often hide under technical scientific names or E-numbers.

  • ✓ Spots hidden gelatin (E441), cochineal / carmine (E120), and shellac (E904).
  • ✓ Flags animal-derived vitamin D3 (lanolin), whey, casein, and bone-char processed ingredients.
  • ✓ Explains why a product is non-vegan by citing each offending ingredient.
Flagged Example
"Skimmed milk powder", "Whey protein", or "E120" instantly trigger a non-vegan status with clear explanations.
🥚

Vegetarian Detection

Meat & Slaughter-Free

Vegetarians consume plant foods along with dairy, eggs, and honey, but need vigilance against slaughterhouse by-products sneakily added to everyday goods.

  • ✓ Identifies animal rennet in cheeses and distinguishes it from microbial or plant rennet.
  • ✓ Detects gelatin in yogurts, puddings, desserts, and confectionery.
  • ✓ Separates fish-derived clarifying agents and animal fats from vegetable shortenings.
Flagged Example
"Traditional rennet" in parmesan or "pork gelatin" in gummy bears immediately alert vegetarian shoppers.
🦧

Palm Oil Detection

Rainforest & Climate

Pioneered by Open Food Facts in 2012, our palm oil detector unmasks palm oil and its numerous cryptic derivatives responsible for tropical deforestation.

  • ✓ Detects unrefined palm oil, fractionated palm oil, and palm kernel oil.
  • ✓ Identifies complex derivatives like palmitate, stearin, and generic "vegetable fats".
  • ✓ Helps protect endangered orangutan and tropical rainforest biodiversity.
Flagged Example
"Hydrogenated palm fat" or "Palmitic acid" alert consumers seeking deforestation-free choices.
Under the Hood

Our Unique Ability to Parse Ingredients

Package ingredient lists are notoriously messy: nested parentheses, multilingual text, percentages, ambiguous trade names, and typos. Here is how Open Food Facts turns chaotic labels into reliable dietary data.

Step 1

Multilingual Tokenization

Our grammar parser understands commas, semicolons, nested parentheses, and percentages across 150+ languages, cleanly separating composite ingredients from their sub-components.

Step 2

Taxonomy Matching

Every recognized term is mapped against the open Ingredients Taxonomy, connecting synonyms, translations, and E-numbers into a standardized global graph.

Step 3

Dietary Rule Evaluation

Each ingredient entry carries explicit status flags (vegan: yes/no/maybe, vegetarian: yes/no/maybe, palm_oil: yes/no/maybe), curated by food scientists and community researchers.

Step 4

Conservative Aggregation

If even one ingredient or sub-ingredient violates a diet, the entire product is flagged. If an ambiguous additive (e.g. E471) could be either plant or animal derived, the status is safely reported as "Unknown".

Good to Know

Frequently Asked Questions

Can the detector catch processing aids like isinglass or bone char?

Because food manufacturers are generally not legally required to print processing aids on packaging (such as fish-bladder isinglass used to clarify certain beers or wines), Open Food Facts can only detect them when the product carries an official certification (e.g. V-Label, Vegan Society) or when the manufacturer voluntarily discloses them. We actively document known wine and beer fining practices in our community wiki.

Why does a product show "Vegan status unknown"?

Certain additives—such as mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids (E471) or lactic acid—can be synthesized from either vegetable oils or animal fats. When the manufacturer does not specify the origin on the label and no third-party vegan certification is present, Open Food Facts conservatively flags the status as "unknown" so consumers can make an informed, cautious decision.

What about "May contain traces of milk or eggs" allergen warnings?

"May contain" statements indicate a risk of unintentional cross-contact in the manufacturing facility, not an intentional recipe ingredient. Open Food Facts differentiates between actual recipe ingredients and cross-contamination alerts, displaying allergen warnings separately so individuals can choose according to their personal risk tolerance.

How can I correct an ingredient mistake or add an unlisted product?

Open Food Facts is a participatory project like Wikipedia. If you spot an error, simply open the product page or use the mobile app to edit the ingredient text, upload a new packaging photograph, or report an issue. Your correction will benefit millions of consumers worldwide immediately!
